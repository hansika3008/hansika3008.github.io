What Counts as the Real Cost of a Purchase?

The price shown on a product page is not always the amount you will spend over the time you own an item. Repairs, maintenance, accessories and resale value can all affect its overall cost.

Understanding these expenses can help you compare products more carefully, especially when one option is cheaper to buy but more expensive to maintain.

1. Purchase price: the starting point

The purchase price is the amount you initially pay for an item. It is usually the easiest cost to identify, but it does not tell you how much the item will cost throughout its useful life.

For example, a laptop costing ₹60,000 may seem expensive compared with one costing ₹45,000. However, the cheaper laptop may have a shorter useful life or higher maintenance expenses. Looking only at the purchase price does not show this difference.

2. Maintenance and repair costs

Some products need regular maintenance to remain useful. These expenses may include servicing, replacement parts, cleaning, repairs or routine upkeep.

  • Laptop: Battery replacement, repairs or servicing.
  • Vehicle: Regular servicing, tyres and replacement parts.
  • Appliance: Repairs, filters and periodic maintenance.
  • Shoes: Cleaning, polishing or minor repairs.

When estimating maintenance, use a realistic yearly amount. If you do not know the exact amount, use a reasonable estimate and remember that actual expenses can vary.

3. Resale value

Some items can be sold after you finish using them. The amount you expect to receive is called the resale value.

For example, if you buy a laptop for ₹60,000 and later sell it for an estimated ₹10,000, that resale amount reduces your estimated net ownership cost.

Resale value is not guaranteed. It depends on the item's condition, age, demand and market prices when you sell it.

4. Calculate an estimated net cost

A simple way to estimate ownership cost is to add the purchase price and expected maintenance, then subtract the expected resale value.

Estimated net cost = Purchase price + Total maintenance − Resale value

Worked example: A laptop

Suppose you purchase a laptop and plan to use it for four years.

  • Purchase price: ₹60,000
  • Estimated maintenance per year: ₹3,000
  • Expected ownership period: 4 years
  • Estimated resale value: ₹10,000

Total estimated maintenance = ₹3,000 × 4 = ₹12,000.

Estimated net cost = ₹60,000 + ₹12,000 − ₹10,000.

Estimated net cost = ₹62,000.

This means the estimated cost of owning the laptop for four years is ₹62,000 under these assumptions. It is not necessarily the amount you will actually spend.

5. Other expenses worth considering

The simple net-cost formula does not include every possible expense. Depending on what you buy, you may also need to consider:

  • Taxes and delivery: Charges added to the advertised price.
  • Accessories: Items needed to use the product properly.
  • Financing: Interest or fees if you purchase using a loan or instalment plan.
  • Replacement: The cost of replacing an item earlier than expected.
  • Opportunity cost: What you could have done with the money instead.

Not every expense applies to every purchase. Include only the costs relevant to your situation, and avoid counting the same expense twice.

6. Why usage and lifespan matter

Two people can buy the same product at the same price but get different value from it. Someone who uses a laptop every day may get more use from it than someone who uses it occasionally.

That is why it can be useful to consider both the estimated net cost and the number of times you expect to use the item.

7. Common mistakes when estimating real cost

  • Comparing products using only their purchase prices.
  • Assuming maintenance and repair costs will always be zero.
  • Overestimating how much an old product can be resold for.
  • Assuming an item will last longer than its realistic useful life.
  • Ignoring how frequently you will actually use the product.

Frequently asked questions

Is real cost the same as purchase price?

No. Purchase price is the initial amount paid. Real cost can also include maintenance, repairs and other ownership expenses, while accounting for any resale value.

Should I always subtract resale value?

If you reasonably expect to sell the item, you can include an estimated resale value. If you do not expect to sell it, use zero. Remember that resale value can change.

Does LifeCost calculate every possible expense?

No. The calculator provides an estimate based on the information entered. Taxes, financing charges, unexpected repairs and other expenses may need to be considered separately.

Is a lower net cost always the better choice?

Not necessarily. Product quality, reliability, safety, warranty, features and personal requirements also matter. Cost calculations are one part of a buying decision.
