How to Calculate Cost Per Use

Cost per use tells you how much an item effectively costs each time you use it. It can help you compare purchases that have different prices, lifespans and usage patterns.

For example, a product with a higher purchase price may have a lower cost per use if you use it frequently and keep it for several years.

What does cost per use mean?

Cost per use is the estimated net cost of an item divided by the total number of times you expect to use it.

Cost per use = Estimated net cost ÷ Total estimated uses

Estimated net cost can account for the purchase price, maintenance over the ownership period and expected resale value.

Step 1: Find the estimated net cost

Start with the amount you pay to purchase the item. Add estimated maintenance expenses over the ownership period, then subtract any expected resale value.

Estimated net cost = Purchase price + Total maintenance − Resale value

Step 2: Estimate the total number of uses

Think about how often you expect to use the item. If you know your weekly usage and expected lifespan in years, you can estimate the total number of uses.

Total estimated uses = Uses per week × 52 × Years of use

This calculation assumes your weekly usage remains approximately the same throughout the ownership period. It is an estimate, not a guarantee.

Step 3: Divide net cost by total uses

Once you have estimated the net cost and total uses, divide the net cost by the number of uses.

Worked example: Laptop

Imagine you buy a laptop for ₹60,000 and expect to use it 20 times per week for four years.

  • Purchase price: ₹60,000
  • Maintenance per year: ₹3,000
  • Expected lifespan: 4 years
  • Expected resale value: ₹10,000
  • Uses per week: 20

Total maintenance = ₹3,000 × 4 = ₹12,000.

Net cost = ₹60,000 + ₹12,000 − ₹10,000 = ₹62,000.

Total uses = 20 × 52 × 4 = 4,160.

Cost per use = ₹62,000 ÷ 4,160.

Estimated cost per use = ₹14.90.

More cost-per-use examples

Example 1: Shoes

Suppose a pair of shoes costs ₹3,000. You expect to use them three times per week for two years, spend ₹200 per year on maintenance and sell them for an estimated ₹500.

  • Net cost = ₹3,000 + (₹200 × 2) − ₹500 = ₹2,900.
  • Total uses = 3 × 52 × 2 = 312.
  • Cost per use = ₹2,900 ÷ 312.

Estimated cost per use = ₹9.29.

Example 2: Gym membership

Suppose a gym membership costs ₹12,000 for one year and you visit four times per week.

  • Annual membership cost: ₹12,000.
  • Estimated visits: 4 × 52 = 208.
  • Cost per visit = ₹12,000 ÷ 208.

Estimated cost per visit = ₹57.69.

If you visit less frequently, the cost per visit increases. This is one reason it is useful to estimate your actual usage instead of assuming you will use a membership every week.

Example 3: Comparing two laptops

Consider two hypothetical laptops. Assume you use both 20 times per week.

Laptop A

  • Purchase price: ₹60,000
  • Maintenance: ₹3,000 per year for four years
  • Resale value: ₹10,000
  • Estimated net cost: ₹62,000
  • Estimated uses: 4,160
  • Cost per use: approximately ₹14.90

Laptop B

  • Purchase price: ₹45,000
  • Maintenance: ₹2,500 per year for three years
  • Resale value: ₹7,000
  • Estimated net cost: ₹45,500
  • Estimated uses: 3,120
  • Cost per use: approximately ₹14.58

In this example, Laptop B has a slightly lower estimated cost per use. However, this does not mean it is automatically the better laptop. Performance, storage, battery life, warranty and your actual requirements should also influence the decision.

When is cost per use helpful?

  • Clothing and shoes: Compare how often you expect to wear an item.
  • Electronics: Consider the expected lifespan and how frequently you use a device.
  • Memberships: Calculate the cost of each gym visit or other activity.
  • Courses: Consider the number of study sessions or times you expect to access the material.
  • Household products: Compare items based on how long they last and how often they are used.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using the purchase price without considering maintenance.
  • Overestimating how often you will use the item.
  • Assuming the product will last longer than expected.
  • Forgetting to account for resale value when it is relevant.
  • Comparing products using different usage assumptions.
  • Choosing an item based only on its cost per use.

Frequently asked questions

Can cost per use be less than one rupee?

Yes. If an item is used many times over its lifespan, its estimated cost per use can be less than ₹1.

What if I do not know the item's lifespan?

Use a reasonable estimate based on the product type, expected usage and available product information. Try different lifespan estimates to see how the result changes.

Should I include maintenance costs?

Include maintenance when it is relevant to the item. For products that need regular servicing or repairs, leaving these expenses out can make the estimated cost look lower than it may be.

Is a lower cost per use always better?

No. A lower figure does not measure quality, comfort, performance, safety or suitability. Consider those factors alongside the calculation.

Is cost per use an exact amount?

No. It depends on estimated lifespan, usage, maintenance and resale value. Actual usage and expenses may be different.
